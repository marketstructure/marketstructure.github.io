  • Mainland insurance capital starts using the Hong Kong ETF channel. One month after the National Financial Regulatory Administration approved insurer access to Hong Kong-listed ETFs through Stock Connect, trading-volume spikes in several ETFs on Monday were widely interpreted as evidence of insurer buying. Some insurers reportedly received detailed operating rules and may already have begun investing, with peers expected to follow. The channel gives an industry with RMB41 trillion (US$6.1 trillion) in assets another route to diversify in Hong Kong, following earlier permission to buy Hong Kong-traded bonds and trade offshore Chinese government-bond futures.

  • China's participating life insurance segment is scaling rapidly amid low rates. Premiums surged 94% year on year in the first half of 2026, pushing the segment past CNY1 trillion (US$149 billion) for the first time, according to Insurance Association of China data. The growth reflects insurers' continued shift toward profit-sharing products as a way to manage guaranteed-rate liabilities while still offering upside tied to insurers' investment performance, a trend reinforced by China Life's own 33.7% rise in new business value to RMB38.2 billion in the first half, aided by stronger equity markets.
  • Hong Kong's 2025 new life-sales race tightened even as the market hit a record. The industry wrote HK$330.9 billion (US$42.2 billion) in new life policies in 2025, up 50.6% on 2024's already-record HK$219 billion. HSBC Life retained the top spot on annualised premiums, but the gap over its nearest rival narrowed sharply from roughly HK$29.3 billion in 2024 to just HK$6.5 billion in 2025, indicating intensifying competition among the leading bancassurance and agency-led insurers for wealth-transfer and high-net-worth business.
 

  • New savings and retirement products emphasise flexibility and legacy planning. In Hong Kong, CTF Life’s Sky Leap 88 savings plan offers an 8%-10% first-year premium discount for customers selecting the Value Enhance Option and paying the basic premium upfront, together with an 8% legacy benefit; the insurer projects a 6.5% internal rate of return by policy year 20. In Thailand, Muang Thai Life’s Flexi Retire annuity series offers multiple premium-payment periods and pension-start ages, annual or monthly income, stepped-up payments and an option to receive 30% of the first pension payment as a lump sum. MTL is also linking annuity payments directly to 44 partner senior-living and nursing-home providers.

  • AI data-centre expansion is both attracting insurer capital and exposing coverage gaps. Nippon Life plans to deploy JPY2 trillion (US$12.75 billion) through infrastructure project finance, including US data-centre construction, citing average spreads above 2% and portfolio diversification. Separately, Howden identified non-damage outages, cyber concentration and construction at operating sites as important gaps in India. More than 90% of facilities have redundant power and cooling systems, yet failures causing no physical damage may not activate conventional cover; parametric solutions are emerging, particularly for retail and wholesale providers, which represent 86% of Indian data centres.
  • Korean insurer dividends remain squeezed by IFRS 17 surrender-value reserve rules despite profit growth. Combined dividends at 10 major life and non-life insurers (Samsung, Hanwha, Kyobo, Shinhan Life, NongHyup, and Samsung, DB, Meritz, Hyundai, KB on the non-life side) fell 17.8% year on year to KRW3.19 trillion, even as underlying earnings held up. The decline stems from surrender-value reserves—non-distributable provisions required when policyholders' surrender value exceeds mark-to-market insurance liabilities—which jumped 25.6% in just six months, from KRW35.04 trillion at end-2025 to KRW44.01 trillion at mid-2026. Regulators have progressively eased the K-ICS threshold that qualifies insurers for reduced reserve-set-aside rates (now 160%, down from 200%, tapering to 130% by 2029), but the reserve burden continues to outpace relief, keeping shareholder returns constrained across the sector.

  • Korean motor underwriting remains loss-making despite the first premium rise in five years. The four largest non-life insurers’ average auto loss ratio was 84.0% in August, improving from 86.7% a year earlier, but the January-August ratio deteriorated by 0.3 percentage points to 84.7%. This remained above the industry’s roughly 80% break-even level after premiums rose only about 1%. The segment recorded a KRW184.8 billion underwriting loss in the first half—its first half-year deficit in six years—as treatment fees and repair-labour costs outpaced premium growth.
