  • China opens the active ETF channel. The CSRC approved the first 18 actively managed ETFs on 19 October after a three-month review, with subscriptions opening 23 October and fundraising capped at RMB2bn per product. Chairman Wu Qing framed the approval at the Financial Street Forum as product innovation intended to bring incremental capital into A-shares after the tech-led sell-off. Beyond the initial capacity of roughly RMB36bn, the format gives mainland managers an exchange-traded wrapper for genuine stock selection for the first time, and the contrast with Korea, where correlation rules on active ETFs are forcing out outperforming products, shows Beijing choosing a more permissive design from the outset. The allocation of the first batch also indicates which houses the regulator is prepared to back in the format.
  • Indonesia's index status becomes a sovereign-fund dispute. MSCI's mid-October warning that Indonesia could be reclassified from emerging market to standalone status over "suspected coordinated trading behaviour" triggered a two-day rout on 19–20 October, compounded by a former trade minister's claim that GIC and Temasek had lobbied for the downgrade. Both Singaporean institutions rejected the allegation as baseless and the Jakarta Composite has recovered about 1.7%. The episode puts the passive eligibility of a major ASEAN benchmark in question and shows how quickly index-governance disputes can be politicised against the region's largest allocators.
  • Vietnam addresses ETF bottlenecks. Draft securities-law amendments published on October 21 would exempt index constituents from an existing 10% investment limit, addressing breaches that can force ETFs to sell holdings or suspend subscriptions. The proposals also broaden the assets accepted in ETF creation and exchange transactions, potentially accommodating futures and gold products, while permitting eligible foreign bank branches to provide public-fund supervisory services. These are changes to portfolio implementation and servicing infrastructure, with the stated objective of reducing constraints on fund growth and foreign participation, rather than simply expanding the product menu.
  • Beijing clarifies offshore trust taxation ahead of grace-period expiry. Mainland authorities have eased uncertainty over the tax treatment of offshore trusts before the current grace period ends, and a US$1.2bn Hong Kong multi-family office reports mainland principals reconsidering where next-generation family members hold tax residence in response. For Hong Kong trust and private-wealth franchises the clarification removes a structuring overhang but also reshapes how cross-border family wealth is domiciled and advised.
  • Chinese quant Lingjun draws US$300 million offshore/ Beijing-based Lingjun Investment’s Hong Kong arm has secured more than US$300 million from a U.S. institutional investor and Southeast Asian clients, with advanced talks underway for a further US$150 million from a Southeast Asian sovereign wealth fund. Fundraising resumes after a two-year pause following 2024 trading restrictions and subsequent risk-control overhauls; the firm’s flagship equity strategy returned more than 70% last year. The inflows illustrate renewed overseas appetite for reformed Chinese quantitative strategies.
  • Asian asset owners shift sustainability from targets and indexes to delivery and risk. Japan's GPIF has broadened its sustainability approach beyond ESG index allocations, outlining the shift in its 2025 Sustainability Investment Report; it revamped its framework for incorporating ESG considerations into investment decisions and began investing in new ESG funds for the first time, with the result that ESG index-based investments declined to 10.6% for domestic equities and 6.5% for foreign equities at the end of March 2026, while the fund brought the previously outsourced Climate VaR analysis and Implied Temperature Rise analysis in-house. Temasek's chief sustainability officer frames the same pivot as a shift in focus toward delivering on climate targets rather than setting them, the fund having already conceded its 2030 portfolio-emissions goal is unlikely while retaining the 2050 net-zero ambition. The common thread is a move by the region's largest allocators away from label- and index-driven sustainability toward active mandates, engagement, in-house climate-risk analytics and underwriting discipline.
